# Tip 1 — Learn AI's Love Language

## Status
**Approved and should lead the episode.**

## Core lesson
AI's “love language” is **specificity and context**, not compliments. A better prompt produces a better workflow because the AI spends less effort guessing what the collector means.

Give it the lane: what you collect, what you exclude, region, platform, physical/digital rules, edition and condition requirements, budget, the output you want, what should be treated as uncertain, and what evidence or verification you expect.

## Comedy beat
Johnny may joke that he should tell the AI it is pretty, thin, loved, his favorite robot, buy it dinner, etc. Jen should riff for a beat, then continue to the educational endpoint. Do not let the joke end the explanation.

Useful riff:
> Very charming, but no. Save that flirting energy. The AI doesn't need compliments; it needs clear direction.

## Bad prompt / good prompt
Bad:
> What rare games should I buy?

Better:
> I collect North American physical PS2 horror games. I want complete black-label copies, not Greatest Hits or reproductions. My budget is about $100 per title. Give me ten targets, explain why each matters to a collector, flag uncertainty, and tell me what I should verify before buying.

## Why it works
A vague prompt forces the AI to infer taste, definitions, exclusions, and output format. The collector then wastes time filtering junk. More context up front reduces cleanup later.

Johnny's useful summary:
> Spend more time creating the prompt to get better results.

Jen's supporting point:
> You're not making the AI smarter; you're making the workflow less messy. Clear in, clear out.

## Pushback and verification
Teach listeners to ask: What are you uncertain about? Why do you think that? What evidence supports it? What would prove this wrong? Is this a fact or a lead? Give me search terms to verify it.

A **lead** says where to look next. A **fact** has evidence you can point to.

> Leads say “go look.” Facts say “here's the receipt.”

# Tip 2 — Make a Better Brain

## Status
**Approved.**

## Core lesson
Collectors often have useful information scattered across Google Docs, Excel spreadsheets, notes, images, screenshots, price notes, and research files. AI can help turn that archive into something documented and searchable in plain language. The collector is often not short on information; they are drowning in information that does not talk to itself.

## Inputs
Depending on the AI product and available connectors: uploaded Excel/CSV files, Google Docs/Sheets through connected Drive access, research documents, notes, and uploaded phone photos or image libraries exposed through a supported connected source. Do not overpromise automatic access to an entire camera roll.

## Plain-language questions
- What PS2 horror games do I own but still need complete?
- Which items are marked high priority but never marked purchased?
- What did I previously write about this variant?
- Which records disagree about region or edition?
- Which games appear in multiple sheets under slightly different names?

## Images as collector records
Photos can become useful reference material: shelf photos, pickup photos, front/back/spine box photos, receipts, variant images, inserts, manuals. AI can extract visible UPCs, product codes, publisher/developer marks, rating logos, dates, edition text, unusual phrases, and other identifiers. Ask it to flag uncertain reads rather than silently guessing.

## Do not merely say “fix my files”
Have AI help build a documented collector system: master field list/schema, field definitions, duplicate report, conflict report, source map showing which file is authoritative for which information, master index, collector glossary, list of missing data, and normalized naming rules.

The goal is to stop maintaining twelve half-systems by memory.

## Questions Jen should proactively ask Johnny
- Are we talking only about the personal collection, or podcast/show research too?
- Are the photos already organized, or is this camera-roll soup?
- What do you want extracted UPCs/product codes to do for you: purchase tracking, variant identification, or smarter searching?
- How broken are the files right now: duplicated, contradictory, incomplete, or simply scattered?

# Tip 3 — Use Photos to Hunt Variants

## Status
**Promising / approved for continued development.**

Sometimes a collector knows a variant exists because they have seen a picture, but the variant is poorly documented and no useful listing title exists. AI can turn the image into research clues: logos, rating marks, sticker wording, product codes, publisher marks, spine differences, region indicators, typography/packaging differences, and partially readable text. Then turn those clues into search terms and verification steps.

The best photos are often the boring ones: straight-on front, back, spine, and well-lit close-ups of codes and stickers. Variants live in boring details.

A blurry photo does not automatically kill the hunt. Change the question from “identify this exact variant” to “what can you see with confidence, what is uncertain, and what clues can we turn into searches for clearer examples?” The AI may be a clue generator rather than the final identifier.

# Tip 4 — Build a Buying Priority List

## Status
**Approved and substantially developed.**

Give AI a list of active collecting targets and use it to help determine what deserves attention first by combining market movement, historical pricing, sales activity, edition-specific context, and current news. Output might be Buy sooner, Watch closely, Safe to wait, or Data too noisy/investigate.

Where data access permits, examine eBay sold comps, PriceCharting or other historical price data, 30/90/365-day movement, volatility, sales count, listing supply, sales velocity/sell-through estimates, outlier sales, wrong editions/regions contaminating comps, and credible catalysts such as reprints, remasters, sequels, adaptations, anniversaries, or delistings.

A dashboard could include game, platform, region, edition, condition target, market estimate, sold range, 30/90/365-day movement, sales count, listing count, velocity, volatility, buy zone, max price, priority, catalyst, data-quality warning, and last updated.

Alerts can be threshold-based: meaningful percentage movement, target enters buy zone, materially changing sales velocity, credible catalyst, or scarce item appears below an instant-buy threshold. Encode buying rules before FOMO hits.

Do not imply perfect direct access to marketplace history. Availability and completeness vary; sell-through may be estimated rather than exact.
